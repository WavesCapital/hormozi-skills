## Find the Real Limit First

Can the business serve more buyers? Can it afford to win them? Do enough good leads buy? Does each buyer leave enough profit? Use facts already in the business profile and its links. Ask only for what could change the next choice. Do not edit the profile as part of a review. (Pages3–6.)

These six stages describe **how marketing works**, not company size. Revenue and headcount are rough examples. A large firm can still lack proof or steady leads. A young firm need not build a full ad team. This map does not replace the separate Scaling Roadmaps. (Pages2,6.)

Page5 has a real layout conflict: the right column says “IF THE ANSWER IS NO,” but the affordability row's text gives a **yes** branch. Follow the explicit condition. If the business can handle and afford more buyers, more demand may fit. Otherwise fix capacity or cash first. Do not let that heading become advice to buy more leads when cash is short.

## More, Better, or New?

Choose the main route that fits time, money and skill. Core Three groups ads, content and outreach. `$100M Leads` splits warm and cold outreach in Core Four. These are different groupings, not grounds to drop either form of outreach. Referrals and affiliates still fit. (Pages7,32; [Leads guide](leads-review.md).)

Do more useful work when the path works and has room. Use the reps to learn and improve. Check the offer, effort, creative and targeting before reflexively switching platforms. A known bad audience or unaffordable loss can change that order. Rule of100 can mean100 minutes **or**100 actions, not a fixed quota for every channel. (Pages7–8,16.)

A new channel can fit saturation, a declining platform or a better expected return after risk and effort. Page32's later channel focus does not ban page8's earlier exceptions. One primary route can have supporting email and a light brand pulse. Page37 says to start that pulse by Stage2, not wait for a large team. (Pages8,16,32,37.)

The book's “always works” and volume multiples are forceful teaching, not measured sales promises for this business. Check quality, results, cost and capacity, not activity alone. (Pages3–4,8.)

## Earn Proof and Steady Demand

Reach people you can serve. Deliver a result worth showing. Agree on what can be recorded and shared. Free or discounted work may help earn early evidence; it does not require a positive review or override site rules on review incentives. Use [proof collection](proof-collection.md#keep-it-true-and-worth-the-cost) for public review asks. Names, footage and customer data need permission. (Pages11–13.)

A lead magnet can reveal a problem, give a trial or solve one part. Pair a steady primary route with useful follow-up. A list needs permission and an easy way to stop messages. Email still costs time and tools. Revenue-share claims and ad credits in this PDF are not forecasts or current provider terms. (Pages14–18.)

Eight weeks of steady leads is a repeatability guide, not a reason to wait before fixing a clear problem. Pick the activity and result measures that fit. (Page18.)

## Make the Customer Math Work

Compare customer acquisition cost with expected lifetime **gross profit**, not lead cost with revenue. Keep forecasts separate from cash collected. Include real delivery and acquisition costs; cheap leads can be expensive buyers. (Pages19–23; [economics guide](offer-economics.md).)

For stable monthly recurring sales, page21 estimates lifetime revenue as monthly price divided by monthly churn, then multiplies by gross margin. Periods must match and churn must be positive. A young group with no observed churn does not imply infinite value or safe ad spend. Software still has costs. Steady-state sales ceilings are not next-month cash. The meal example loses eight dollars after ten dollars of ads despite twenty dollars of revenue.

Possible levers are price, a useful premium, more help, a next offer, a quick early win and better-fit buyers. Close-rate price bands are test ideas, not proof of underpricing. Check lead quality, enough sales and profit after the change. Early-contact timing is an onboarding example. Prepay brings cash forward but leaves service owed. (Page22.)

Keep these examples straight:

- 20% buying an **extra** offer at5 times base price yields `1 + 0.2 × 5 = 2` times base revenue. If that premium **replaces** the base for20%, the result is `0.8 + 0.2 × 5 = 1.8`. State which structure applies. (Page22.)
- Gross margin rising67% to80% means about19.4% more gross profit at the same revenue, not automatically twice the profit. Net-profit doubling needs overhead assumptions. The80% service target is not universal. (Page23.)
- A3:1 expected lifetime gross-profit-to-customer-cost ratio is a guide, not permission to scale. Cash timing, refunds, retention uncertainty and room to deliver still matter. (Pages21,23.)

## Test Before Scaling

Separate finding winners from expanding winners. Test distinct angles and formats. Optimize for the deepest useful action with enough reliable data, such as good meetings or purchases—not cheap signups that do not buy. (Pages24–28.)

Page26's kill rule is ambiguous: “ad cost” reaching3 times target cost per qualified lead does not clearly say total spend versus unit cost or how many outcomes occurred. Do not silently add “with zero leads” and call that the source's rule. Define cost, outcomes, conversion delay and affordable loss before choosing a stop rule. A profitable ad is not bad just because total spend crossed a small threshold.

Do not repair the source by silently changing its left side to **cost per qualified lead**, either. That is one possible interpretation, not what the printed equation explicitly says. State the uncertainty, calculate actual cost per good lead, and judge the test using outcomes and cash limits. Label a proposed stop rule as your recommendation, not a quote from page26.

The70/30 allocation is **money** for proven winners versus tests; it assumes winners exist. Hooks'70/20/10 creative-effort split uses different buckets and units. Neither is universal. Daily25% budget rises, ad-count targets and bridge-to-profit stories do not guarantee safe spend. Keep real cash limits. The source does not authorize spending or an ad change. (Pages26–27.)

## Build Useful Output and Trust

Once testing works, remove the production limit. Combine hooks, bodies and calls to action; vary genuine winners and reuse useful work with permission. Count the actual choices when multiplying assets. More output matters only if it helps get good buyers. (Pages29–33.)

Make human work reliable, write down how it works, then use agents where helpful. Check their output. The book's4–8x speed claims and error-free language do not prove agents cannot fail. Do not feed private customer data into tools without authority. Use sales feedback to learn which content brings valuable buyers, not just views. (Pages30–33.)

Useful information, a steady promise and proof can build trust. Total acquisition cost, buyer value, referrals, branded searches and buyer reports can complement direct tracking. Missing attribution does not prove free demand or a brand-caused sale. Long-form and live interaction can deepen trust; this does not broaden the Goal's no-Shorts scope. (Pages34–37.)

Page36 labels its brand quote **paraphrased**; keep that label. Credit Preston and Maddox where named. Do not turn their words into invented first-person Alex stories. Page40's workshop pitch is context, not a required purchase. (Pages27,31,36,40.)

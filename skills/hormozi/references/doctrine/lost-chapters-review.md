These chapters add depth to the books. Some were cut because they are risky, complex, or useful to fewer firms. Treat them as choices, not required steps. The original has some math and diagram errors. Keep the original intact and explain a conflict when it affects the answer.

## Choose Buyers And Offers That Fit

- **Pages11–17:** Learn from customers who buy more, stay longer, and are good to serve. Improve the product OR choose buyers it already fits. More leads are not always worth more.
- **Pages21–44:** Free and deep discounts may raise response. Premium offers can work too. Match the buyer, offer, message and sales path. Added forms, meetings or content can lose good buyers; use friction to solve a real fit or capacity problem.
- **Pages75–92:** Think of an offer grid, not a ladder every buyer must climb. People may enter later or buy related help at the same price. Start with a useful next need you can serve well. Do not build a complex funnel just because the drawing has one.
- **Pages93–99:** Teach enough for buyers to understand the offer. A complex sale may need more time. The event close rates use different counts: people in the room, people on the call at the pitch, registered people, or final-day attendees. Do not swap those bases or make them promises.

## Keep The Promise And The Money Clear

Read the full section that fits:

| Choice | Pages | What can change the advice |
|---|---|---|
| Freemium | 101–104 | Free users still cost money. It needs cheap delivery, repeat value, a paid next need and funds to reach it. It is an acquisition method, not the whole business. |
| Pick your price | 105–109 | The free version stays free when the buyer pays nothing. Paid tiers are optional. The gallery price range is a separate paid version. |
| Another revenue stream | 111–116 | Paired: free A requires buying B. Independent: free A does not require B. The second stream must cover the cost of both. |
| Rewards for staying | 117–123 | Choose when: time OR a milestone. Choose what: a one-time bonus, changing bonus, or lasting upgrade. Keep it useful and affordable. Public praise needs the customer's consent. |
| Lifetime discount | 125–131 | Percent off, dollars off and a fixed price are different promises. Rising costs can hurt a fixed price. A bounded price lock may fit better. The retail price and deadline must be real. |
| Discount plus fee | 133–136 | Show the full first bill and later bills. A fee can erase the advertised saving. Explain real work; do not hide charges behind a discount. |

Check the amount, time and base before using an example. Pages45–73 separate acquisition cost, lifetime gross profit and time to earn back the cost. Customer-funded acquisition means using customer receipts to fund marketing; it is not consumer purchase financing. Cash receipts, gross profit and net profit are not the same thing.

Some useful source checks:

- Page80 mixes receipts from several offers and uses a different buyer count from page79. Its written totals also disagree. It cannot prove a safe increase in ad spend.
- Page103's five cents per free user per month, with a 1% paying share, means $5 of monthly service cost per payer. It is not a one-time $5 customer acquisition cost.
- Page115's 80% × $300 × 80% gives $192 of expected upsell gross contribution before free delivery and acquisition costs.
- Page117 says three free months equals 25% off, but draws three free months beside a 12-month paid commitment. Three free within 12 total is 25%; three free plus 12 paid is 20% over 15 total. The same page's $200/$600 is 33.33%, not its 1–5% label.
- Page133 shows $24 plus a $99 fee: $123 to start, then $200. Page135 uses separate examples: $100 plus $1900 is $2000; $120 plus $1000 plus two $1000 bills is $3120.
- Page152 says acquisition cost is “at least” one third of lifetime profit. For a 3:1 gross-profit-to-cost target, the cost must be **at most** one third. Check the ratio, not that reversed phrase.

## Find The Team Limit

Pages137–138 show sales held back by hiring. Ten screens per week, one in ten sent to sales, and one in four hired means about one hire every four weeks. If one rep leaves every four weeks, the team only replaces losses. Check that path before rewriting a pitch that works. Group interviews helped that trainable frontline role; they are not the right screen for every job.

Pages141–144 map customer stages to hiring. Keep the four methods separate from the people who use them. Warm outreach, cold outreach, content and paid ads are methods. Referrals, community owners and recruiters are helpers. The diagram does not replace a firm's own CRM stages.

Pages145–150 explain how to build skill:

1. **Document:** Write the steps and test them yourself.
2. **Demonstrate:** Show the work. Fix unclear steps.
3. **Duplicate:** Watch the worker do it. Give fast feedback on one step at a time.

“I understand” is not a demonstration. Practice may be needed even when the steps are clear. If someone follows bad directions, fix the directions; do not punish them for following them.

The Performance Diamond checks four causes: unclear expectations, missing skill, low motivation, and something blocking the work. Ask what changed. Have them show the work. Check supplies and tools. Do not assume they are lazy. Use pages151–153 to separate poor lead fit, too few good leads, and a sales problem; count all acquisition costs.

## Match Meetings To The Work

Read147–150 alongside155–161. Short frontline huddles and weekly coaching serve a different job from deep maker work. Six to eleven check-ins is not six to eleven long meetings for every worker.

Group useful meetings and admin. Protect larger work blocks. Set clear reply times and check actual output. Managers should weigh the cost of an interruption; makers still need useful meetings. The author's 4am start and after-1pm meetings are examples, not rules. Quiet time may fit some teams, not the whole firm. Keep needed customer care covered.

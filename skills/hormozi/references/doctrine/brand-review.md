# Build a Name Buyers Trust

Use this for brand and trust questions. Also use it to review content fit or a change in the target buyer. It expands `HZ-DEM-008`. Use `HZ-SYN-004`: deep thought, plain words. Do not force this full review onto a small copy task.

## Find the Real Gap

A brand is what people link your name with. A logo can mark that link. It cannot create trust by itself.

Check three things:

- **Reach:** How many of the right people know you?
- **Influence:** How much does knowing you change what they do?
- **Direction:** Does it move them toward buying, or away?

More views can spread a bad name. Good work can stay unknown. Find which gap matters here. A rise in views alone does not prove a stronger brand. Check buyer response and sales. Do not assume one post caused a change when other things changed too.

Source: Branding Playbook, PDF pages 9–13. The page 11 grid separates reach from brand quality. Page 12 plots influence and direction. Its named examples show the author's own views, not measured scores for all buyers.

## Build the Right Link

1. Name the buyer. Do they need it? Can they pay? Can you reach them? Is that market growing? The person who uses the product may not be the person who buys it.
2. Find what that buyer wants and trusts. Use their words and actions. Mark guesses as guesses.
3. Make useful work that connects your name with those things. This may be content or a product. It may be a service or a real trusted partner.
4. Share it with those buyers. Keep what gets the right response. Improve the rest.

Check what the brand means to them now. Then choose the link it needs to earn. Do not assume your chosen words are what buyers believe.

Source: PDF pages 22–25 and 36. The care-home example mixes buyer and end-user clues; check the actual buying role. Pages 17–20 show how learned links can add value. They do not grant permission to imply a false partner or endorsement.

## Keep the Promise

The source shows three layers of trust:

- What the business says.
- What other people say about it.
- What the buyer sees and gets firsthand.

That last layer shapes what buyers tell others. Good work can earn repeat sales and referrals. Poor work can undo the ad's promise and hurt the person who made the referral too.

If the work falls short, fix that gap. A new logo or louder ads will not fix a broken promise. Earn real proof from the work. Do not make up reviews or hide faults with praise.

Source: PDF pages 26–27. The curved arrows on page 26 show the feedback loop. This is a model of buyer trust, not a rule that hearsay outranks business records.

## Grow or Change the Brand

You have five paths. Serve bigger or smaller buyers. Move to a nearby group. Go broader or narrower. Match your content and proof to the group you choose. Narrowing can improve fit without making the total market larger.

For a change, ask who you gain and lose. Who stays with you? Compare their likely value and the cost to serve them. Do not just count followers. More of what works may be a better bet than a full change. Do not let a few angry comments overrule strong buyer results either.

Source: PDF pages 28–31. Page 30's circles show gains and losses with shared buyers in the overlap. They are a concept sketch, not measured data. Using buyer value to judge the trade applies the profit aim on page 20.

## Keep the Math and Claims Honest

Brand work costs time and money. Count that cost. Test whether the extra profit pays for it. Build trust over time while keeping a sales path the business can afford.

The source has limits worth checking:

- PDF page 20 has an error: 100 clicks at 4% gives **four sales**, not two. Four sales at $2,000 gross profit each gives $8,000. Two would give $4,000.
- Page 21 calls the unbranded price $2,000; the table says $2,500. The 50% lift fits the change from 4% to 6%, not the full sales gain from four to 18. Check which inputs you use.
- The return example leaves out brand-building costs. Its 11x claim is not a forecast for this business.
- The Pepsi story on pages 14–16 gives no sales data. Do not present its causal claim as an independently proved result.
- Page 16 says a strong brand need not stir up hate. Page 15 claims any publicity beats none. That is an opinion, not a safe rule for every firm.
- Pages 32–33 discuss recovery through good work. Still fix unresolved harm. Page 36's five-year line calls for patience. It does not call for five years of losses or promise success.

Keep the original source intact. Explain a flaw when it affects the answer. Do not repeat all these cautions in each reply.

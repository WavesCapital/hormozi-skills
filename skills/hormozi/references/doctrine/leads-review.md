# Use the Full Leads Method

Use this guide for lead flow, content, outreach, referrals, agencies, partner programs or the team that runs them. Read the part that could change the advice. It does not require a full review for each short question.

Source: `$100M Leads: How to Get Strangers to Want to Buy Your Stuff`, supplied 385-page edition. The source key is `100m-leads-how-to-get-strangers:29823024eeb1`. Page numbers below are physical PDF pages. Use that exact key with `corpus.py query --source-key`; then open each result with `read`. A similarly named chapter or master file is not this book.

## Find the Real Gap

A name you can contact is a lead. A person who shows interest is an engaged lead. That does not yet make them a fit, a booked call or a paid buyer. Keep each count on its own basis. A reply can be negative.

Start with the step that holds back the result. Do not buy more names when good names go untouched. Do not blame the ad when the right buyers reach a call but the sale fails. Check the offer, buyer fit and sales work before choosing a fix.

The four direct methods cross warm or cold audiences with private or public contact: warm outreach, free content, cold outreach and paid ads. Tools can send private messages faster; that does not turn them into public posts. Customers, staff, agencies and partners can run these methods too. They are people who bring leads, not a fifth direct method.

## Offer Value Before Adding Steps

Try the core offer first when buyers are ready. A lead magnet helps when a narrow useful result builds trust or reveals the next need. It should solve its small problem, not hide a sales call behind a claim of free help.

Choose the problem and buyer first. Then choose a sample, a way to reveal a problem, or one step in a larger job. Software, useful facts, a service or a physical item can deliver it. Test the name and promise, make it easy to use, then give a clear next step. Include delivery and sales costs when judging its value.

Free work for an early group can buy practice, feedback and proof. It is not a rule to serve everyone free or use a fixed group size. If free gets no interest, check the desired result, trust and hidden work before cutting price again.

## Match the Message to the Person

For warm outreach, use real shared context. Ask a clear, low-pressure question. Do not fake a friendship. Keep giving useful help and respond to signs of interest.

For content, hook attention, hold it, and reward it with the promised value. Those jobs can fit in one line or span a long piece. Lists, steps and stories work differently. Use real events and lessons; do not invent a personal story. A useful long piece can build trust. Short is not always better.

Give more than you ask. The book prefers giving until people ask, but it allows an earlier offer when cash is needed. An offer can appear in the first post. One clear action helps the reader know what to do. One platform can build skill with less strain; many need more time and people.

For cold outreach, check list quality, whether people notice the message, and whether the offer earns interest. Use relevant facts to make it personal. Give real value fast when you can afford to. Track attempts, interest, fit, calls and sales. Respect a no and a request to stop. An old unanswered sequence is not a license for endless contact.

For ads, call out the right buyer, show the value and name the next step. Match that step to the real page and process. Keep the look and promise clear across the handoff. Test strong openings, but make sure the body pays off the hook.

## Use Plain Words Without Losing the Idea

Page 169 gives third-grade guidance for cold writing. Page 211 says to assume the reader is new and rushed. Page 210 says to stop polishing once copy no longer holds back growth.

Keep the idea deep and the words easy. A shorter claim that changes who qualifies, what they get or when it happens is not a better line. The book's reported reply gain is an example, not a promised lift for this business. Follow [the shared writing rule](../communication-standard.md).

## Choose More, Better or New

Do more of a working method when more useful output is feasible. Ask what blocks ten times the current work. Fix that limit when needed. More effort is not a cure for an offer that loses money or a team that cannot serve the buyers.

Test a change where it can improve the whole result. The lowest rate is a clue, not proof of the best fix. Watch later steps too. A lift in replies that brings worse buyers may not help. A week or a few tests in an author's example is not enough evidence for every firm.

Compare the likely gain from a new route with more work on the current one. A real risk may also call for a new route. The rough order is a new placement, platform, then method. Another placement does not remove the risk of losing that whole platform. See [organizing-growth.md](organizing-growth.md) for that case.

The Rule of 100 sets steady daily work. Page 235 asks for 100 minutes making ads, with a separate budget based on costs and returns. The recap image on page 366 labels ads in dollars instead. These disagree. That short label does not set a fixed $100 daily spend. Outreach counts contacts; content counts minutes of work, not 100 posts.

Open-to-goal focuses on a result, not just attempts. Use a clear daily aim and a work block that fits the person. Respect spend limits, consent and the need for rest. Keep reported results honest. Page 364 says early waking has no magic and allows a shorter block.

The ad roadmap on pages 369–374 shows common stages, not a required org chart. Do not confuse it with the separate company Scaling Roadmaps. Level 7 is not filled in. The dice story teaches you to keep trying. But its free retries do not model real business risk.

## Earn Referrals, Then Ask

Build goodwill with the right customers, a promise you can keep and useful wins along the way. Study what the best customers do. Test those actions with new customers. Fix common service problems and check whether the fix works before spreading it.

A quiet referral stream does not prove a bad product. Have you asked? Does the customer see a benefit worth risking a friend's trust? A clear introduction and a relevant reward may help more than a price cut.

Name who gets the reward and which completed action earns it. A two-sided reward splits the total budget; it does not quietly double it. In the Dropbox image on page 276, the friend must join and install. An invitation alone does not earn the reward. Keep requests for reviews honest and disclose rewards when needed.

Count referred paying customers against customer losses on the same time basis. Referral share, the share of customers who refer, and growth over a base are different measures. Lifetime profit from referrals does not prove this month's ads are paid for.

## Get Help That Can Do the Work

Model hiring and losses together. One hire every four weeks cannot grow the team if one person leaves every four weeks. That is a flow rate, not a claim that each person stays four weeks.

Train with document, demonstrate and duplicate. Write working steps, show them, then watch a learner follow them. Fix a faulty step. If the person knows the steps but lacks speed, practice may be the need. Correct one thing at a time; do not take over every attempt.

An agency can run the work or help you learn it. The author's preferred learning-and-handoff plan is not a ban on keeping a good agency. The image on page 310 allows firms or owners to keep an agency if they want. Agree on any teaching or transfer plan up front. Compare real results before cutting useful support.

For a complex team, relevant leaders may matter more than cheap potential. This does not make every early role a senior hire. Compare payroll and other acquisition costs with actual paid results.

## Build a Partner Offer and a Lasting Pay Path

Find businesses that already serve the right buyers. The offer to a partner is a useful earning path; it is not just the end customer's product. Check the value, work, trust and time from their side.

Separate recruiting, training, first sales and steady sales. A signup is not an active partner. A launch can help them start. Prepare the assets, build interest, reveal value, then give a clear action. Use real dates and rewards. The whisper-tease-shout pattern can work beyond partners; its exact book-launch timing is not a fixed rule.

Keep sales going by fitting into the partner's usual customer flow. They can give away your lead magnet, sell it and keep that income, or sell the main offer for shared income. Pick the arrangement that works for both sides. Include the cost to serve it. See [launch-review.md](launch-review.md) for a dated kit, not the whole partner model.

Choose rewards for the result wanted. Paying for an early step needs evidence that it leads to sales. State the pay basis, qualifying action and timing. Product rewards can cost less than their value to the partner, but high-volume partners may prefer cash. Paid training, bulk buys, three tiers and uncapped pay are source examples or preferences, not terms to impose on every firm.

Source: pages 316–349.

## Check the Money, Not Just the Printed Ratio

Gross profit means sales less the cost to serve those sales. Compare that profit over a customer's life with the full cost to win that customer. Keep overhead and early cash needs visible. Page 220 calls 3:1 an observed guide, not a rule. Early cash must cover both the cost to win buyers and serve them before it funds more growth.

The source contains math and wording errors. Do not repeat them as teaching:

- Page 298 reverses the one-third cost limit. A 3:1 gross-profit-to-cost target needs cost at most one-third of gross profit.
- Pages 329–331: a $200 price less $40 delivery leaves $160 gross profit. A $40 payout gives 4:1, not 3:1. The $120 left divided by $40 is 3:1, but that is a different measure. An average payout of $30 cuts $40 by 25%, not 33%.
- Pages 346–347: $120,000 sales less $30,000 delivery and $36,000 partner pay leaves $54,000. That is before overhead and the cost to recruit the partner. Dividing $54,000 by $4,000 gives 13.5, not the printed 12.5. This is a partner-level return, not the cost to win an end customer.
- Pages 361–362: 300 flyers versus 150,000 over a 30-day campaign is 1/500, not 1/1500. At a 0.5% response rate, 300 flyers give 1.5 expected replies, not a guaranteed count.

Recalculate an example on a clear basis. Explain the correction briefly when it changes the advice. Do not alter the original source or mistake lifetime returns for cash on hand.

# Choose a Price That Pays

Writing rule: `HZ-SYN-004`. Keep the math exact and the words easy.

Read this for prices, payment terms, fees, tiers or paid cover. These are choices to test, not ten steps every firm must take. Start with the buyer's need and the firm's main goal.

For existing buyers and price-change notices, read [price-change.md](price-change.md). It covers testing, loyalty terms and the RAISE letter without invented promises.

## Judge the Whole Return

More yeses is not the goal by itself. Nor is the biggest first bill. Compare what the same pool of leads can earn over the full relationship.

Gross profit means sales less the cost to deliver them. For a simple repeat service:

```text
gross profit per buyer = (price per period - service cost per period) × paid periods
gross profit per lead = buy rate × gross profit per buyer
```

Use the actual cash and cost pattern when it changes over time. Subtract lead costs and added overhead to see what the firm keeps. Show cash timing apart from profit. Cash collected now may pay for work still owed.

One price can win with fewer buyers. A longer term can help cash but hurt sales. Weigh those effects; they do not all need to improve at once. A tiny modeled edge is not proof of a lasting win. Compare like groups and separate known results from guesses.

## Set Price From Value, Not Fear

Costs tell you what the offer must cover. Rival prices show what buyers may compare. Neither alone tells you what your offer is worth to the buyer. Learn what they want and make the value real.

Full capacity or a strong close rate can be reasons to test a higher price. They do not prove that price is the main problem. Count lost sales and the cost of serving those who stay. Rent does not fall just because buyer count falls.

Use tiers when buyers need different levels and the team can serve them well. A high-price option must be real, useful and worth delivering. Do not choose it from sales totals alone. Include the time and costs of bespoke work.

## Choose the Terms That Fit

| Choice | What to weigh |
|---|---|
| Bill every four weeks | Compare yearly spend, not just the unchanged bill amount. |
| Annual or quarterly prepay | Weigh cash now against discounts, buyer drop-off and work still owed. |
| An agreed yearly rise | Match real costs and value. State the actual rate or index rule. |
| Round the price | Test buyer response. A price ending is not a universal win. |
| Annual fee plus monthly bills | Show total spend and when each fee is due. Give real value for the fee. |
| Backup payment method | Recover failed payments with consent. Measure what it saves. |

A small daily or monthly price can make a large bill easier to grasp. Show the full charge and term clearly too. Four-week billing is a price increase over calendar-month billing at the same amount. Do not hide that change.

For a 52-week comparison, count 13 four-week periods or 52 weekly periods. A fifth prepaid 12-week bill may include next year's service. Keep receipt dates apart from the service period. Compound yearly rises over the actual number of raises.

A fixed increase is not a CPI adjustment unless the terms truly use that index. CPI is a measure of price changes. Honor promised price protection and existing contracts.

Fee and fee-waiver paths differ. Do not count both benefits for the same buyer. Card rules and tax duties depend on the facts and place. The book's fee, state map and sample tax code are not current legal authority. Tax held for the government is not profit.

## Sell Useful Ongoing Help

Keep a smaller service after the main job when it still helps the buyer. Set its price from that value and its real cost. Agree to the charge and start date up front. Keep any access already promised.

The book's example adds $3,600 gross profit per buyer who takes the ongoing plan. Half take it, so it adds $1,800 per original buyer on average. It is not $1,800 in cash today or profit for every subscriber. Include take rate and duration when you compare plans.

## Price the Risk You Take

Paid cover needs a clear promise and remedy. State what the buyer gets and what it costs. Keep rights and promises they already have. Get consent before charging.

Model claim cost, not just the share who never claim. A fee refund and a full replacement have different costs. Include the relevant labor, shipping and repeat claims. A fee equal to one replacement does not make the plan risk-free.

Study averages and author stories are not a forecast for this firm. The graphs on pages 16–17 use different samples and incomplete labels. Do not add the ten-play totals on pages 27 and 61 into a promised gain.

Pick the best-fitting change from expected return, work and risk. Set a test tied to the actual uncertainty. Do not invent a fixed sample size or test length. Keep any new number clearly labeled as a proposed choice.

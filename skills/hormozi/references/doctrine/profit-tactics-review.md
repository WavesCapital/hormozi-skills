## Win the Right Buyers

Tactics **1–3, 5–11** support the [demand guide](demand.md), [offer guide](offer-economics.md) and [writing rule](../communication-standard.md).

- Make the free offer truly useful, then qualify who gets costly help. A five-hour free call for a good-fit buyer is not free consulting for everyone. Choose an edge buyers care about—speed, less risk, ease or a sustainable price—and build the work to back it up. Lead with the result the buyer wants, then deliver the work it takes. (1–3.)
- When ad spend stops working, check creative, the free offer and value per buyer. Cutting the cost to make ads only helps if that is a real limit. A permitted avatar of the firm's own speaker can be tested; it is not a license to use someone else's identity or assume the ads work equally well. (5–6.)
- A newsletter can have a familiar subject, a short opening, one useful idea with an example and one ask. Fresh content can use a steady format. Do not force that newsletter shape, a joke or a meme into a short reply. (7.)
- Branded search may reach buyers who are already looking. Check whether the paid ad adds sales beyond those who would come anyway. A long page or video can help buyers who need an explanation, proof and answers. Shorter is not always better. (8–9.)
- Make every kind of copy easy to read. Tactic 10 adds direct audio support to the existing third-grade writing rule. Its follow-up transcript resolves a garbled phrase as shorter sentences; it is not a new word-count law. Keep facts and conditions when cutting words. (10, 960–1071 seconds.)
- Test the weak part of the path. Include forms, booking tools and video players, not just words. Keep changes separable when learning what caused the gain. A weekly test rhythm is not proof a test has enough buyers. (11.)

Tactic 9 contains two different results: recovering from an 80% drop returns a page from one-fifth to its old level, a 5× recovery. The separate 20% lift is over a control. Do not call the recovery a new 5× gain over the old control. Reported gains elsewhere are source stories, not forecasts.

## Show Honest Proof

Tactics **4, 16–19** connect to [proof review](proof-review.md) and [proof collection](proof-collection.md).

Place a clear, useful message where buyers notice it. A sign's benefit and placement matter; the source's sign story does not justify blocking an exit. Show genuine proof near the sale. Ask for a real, specific story, including a hard moment only if there was one. Do not write a fake low point for an already successful buyer. Many clips of one result still come from one result. (4, 16–17.)

The source also gives review-for-merchandise and five-star employee-bonus scripts. Keep these as historical examples, not ready-to-send defaults. A reward tied to posting is still an incentive even if the ask says “honest.” An employee bonus tied to five stars differs from neutral feedback. Use the linked channel rules before writing a public-review ask; do not copy old Google or Yelp tactics as present permission. (4, 18–19.)

## Help Interested Leads Buy

Tactics **12–14, 34–45** add to [sales](sales.md), [lead nurture](lead-nurture-review.md) and [closing](closing-review.md).

- Make follow-up useful: answer a question, show relevant proof or address a real concern. The source's first 3 days, next 7 days and next 30 days are successive periods, not a single 30-day calendar. Adapt timing to actual interest and channel permission. (12.)
- A missed-call message needs a real callback owner and a promise the team can keep. A competitor voicemail needs a true, useful observation, not fake urgency. Tactic 34's fast contact follows an opt-in; it does not time the first touch on each newly researched cold lead. (13–14, 34.)
- Explain a belief and its cost without attacking the buyer. Show real benefits gained and real costs avoided; never create threats or invent the seller's past experience. (35–36.)
- Agree on the next useful meeting while together. Do not add a meeting when the sale can finish now. A discovery call, matched video and decision call can reduce repeated pitches, but may also add friction. Compare the whole path. (37, 45.)
- Put price in context without dodging a direct price question. Keep the cost of the problem and the fee on the same time basis. A daily equivalent is not a daily payment plan. The full problem cost is not all proven savings. (38–40.)
- Give people space to think after the ask. The suggested seconds and claimed study results are not a universal mental timer. Give a real, useful demo, not a forecast from one example. A yes to fit is not consent to price, terms or purchase. (41–43.)
- Negotiate with real choices, acceptable trades and honest alternatives. Anchoring first or offering multiple options can help; neither is a law. Do not invent competing offers or a property's future value. (44.)

## Improve Price and Cash

Tactics **15, 20–24, 50–54, 56, 58** add to [pricing](pricing-review.md) and [money models](money-models-review.md).

Reduce the specific feared loss with a remedy you can fund and deliver. Repairs or continued support may fit better than a refund. Calling a promise insurance does not make it an insurance policy. (15.)

Premium positioning can justify dropping a weak budget tier. That does not ban free offers or a useful entry product. Test front-end prices by later buyer value and costs too. Tactic 21's 47/49/97 figures conflict, including wrong subtraction, in both saved passes. Keep the test idea; do not use those figures as verified price proof. (20–21.)

Peak-time prices or paid speed can fit when they solve a real capacity problem. Protect current promises and count extra work. Reversing a test does not erase lost trust or contract duties. Close-rate bands can suggest a price test, not calculate an optimum; lead quality, cost, demand and sample size matter. (22–24.)

- Disclose the whole annual fee, not just the monthly headline. At $39 × 12 plus $99, annual receipts rise from $468 to $567: about 21.15% more revenue, not necessarily profit. (50.)
- Keep extra purchases separate from replacement tiers. One hundred $100 buyers plus 20 extra $500 purchases plus 4 extra $2,500 purchases gives $30,000. Uptake and the ability to serve those buyers are assumptions. (51.)
- The 80% service gross-margin target has a stated recurring-hourly-labor exception. Going from 80% to 90% at the same revenue adds 10 percentage points, or 12.5% more gross profit—not double profit. The audio's missing-thousands error stays flagged. (52–53.)
- The 3:1, 6:1, 9:1 and 12:1 buyer-value-to-acquisition-cost buffers depend on how much work remains manual. They are source scaling guides, not universal funding gates. Count labor, cash timing, retention risk and delivery. (54.)
- Compare improving one rate with a larger change in reachable demand or output. Neither traffic nor staff time is limitless. A 10× result is 1,000% of the old result, not a 1,000% increase. (56.)
- Durable earnings may add to a firm's sale value. A valuation is not cash in the bank, and reinvestment does not make it automatically tax-free. Tactic 58's tax rhetoric is not a basis for tax advice. (58.)

## Help Buyers Stay and Buy Again

Tactics **25–32, 55, 59–60** add to [customer value](customer-value.md) and [retention](retention-review.md).

Give a reason to stay, not just a reason to join. Put a useful reward before the observed point people leave; average tenure does not show that point by itself. Plan retention, feedback, referrals and the next offer around real value. A win can justify an ask; a purchase alone is not a result. (25–26.)

Support should solve the issue, gather the missing facts or pass it to a real owner. A fast acknowledgement is not a fast resolution. Give an honest next step you can meet. For payment plans, early value and clear progress may help; they do not guarantee payment or authorize collecting another firm's loans. (27–28.)

Make a welcome extra truly extra. Do not hide an already promised benefit and present it as a gift. Model the cost of rewards, skipped months, credits and refunds. An example firm's fifth-month gift does not prove its actual churn peak. (29–30.)

Reactivation starts with real past interest, not a scraped stranger. Test the short question and timing with that relationship in mind. Before cutting a low-use feature, check infrequent but vital uses and what buyers were promised. Two weeks without complaints does not prove no one needs it. (31–32.)

Repeat buying and productive partner channels can both make growth last. Neither means every product needs a subscription. A useful extra around a cost center can earn money without removing a promised basic service. Disclosed affiliate tools may fit onboarding, but buying and staying are not guaranteed. Tactic 60's example yields $320 initially and $120 per later month only if all stated purchases and recurring payments happen; costs remain. (55, 59–60.)

## Fix the Work That Holds Growth Back

Tactics **33, 46–49, 57, 61–70** add to [diagnosis](diagnose.md) and [team work](team-operations.md).

Too many markets, weak prices, poor pay economics, too many sites or too many firms can split focus. Check the actual issue before cutting pay, people or locations. Shorter work cycles help when decisions wait too long; more meetings do not create twice the capacity. Give priority work a clear result, owner and due time after checking current commitments. Explain how a role creates or protects value; an unclear answer is a coaching need, not proof the role is worthless. Give observable start/stop/keep feedback, not a personality label. (33, 46–49.)

Compare a partner's needed time, expertise, assets and money with hiring or buying that help. Chemistry or money alone does not prove equity is the best choice. (57.)

Find the hard capability this business needs now, not the problem the owner likes solving. More demand helps only where there is room to serve it. Tactic 62's “never both” is too broad across products and time slots. Both saved passes mislabel a restaurant without empty tables; keep the clear definitions and flag that example. A full calendar suggests testing price or capacity, not an automatic 50% rise. (61–62, 5689–5856 seconds.)

Fast, sound work and patient foundations can coexist. Do not skip training or overload the team to meet an arbitrary date. The source names network-effect markets as an exception to its broad patience advice. A founder's willingness to keep running the firm matters, but burnout does not justify impulsive firing or spending. One growth priority does not cancel delivery, safety or payroll. (63–65.)

An expensive hire can exceed the cash left by a growing firm. At $2m revenue and 20% net margin, a fully added $250k–$300k hire consumes 62.5%–75% of the old $400k profit. Compare runway, scope and alternatives; the source's revenue band does not require that hire. (66.)

Keep useful persistence without contempt for other choices. Unusual effort does not prove a promised income rank. Separate taste or insults from a real customer problem. Shared purpose can help work matter; a parable is not a measured productivity result. Patient mastery still allows stopping a failed tactic. (67–70.)

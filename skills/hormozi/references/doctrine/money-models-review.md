## Start With the Need

Find what stops the next good sale. Is it weak value, low trust, a cash gap, poor timing, or the wrong scope? Choose the offer that solves that problem. Do not make a new product just to fill a box.

The book groups offers into four families. First, get a buyer. Next, offer the next useful thing. After a no, offer another fit. For repeat value, earn repeat payments. A business can use one, several or all. Build one reliable step at a time. The author aims to earn early cash within thirty days. He still allows a later offer when that timing fits best. (Pages23–25,150,153–155.)

Check both sides of the deal. Will the buyer get value? Can the business deliver it and keep enough cash? A partner's useful product may fill a gap for a commission. That avoids building the product, not all work or risk. Use actual partner terms. (Page154.)

## Get the First Good Sale

| Offer | When it may help | Keep this clear |
|---|---|---|
| Win your money back | The buyer needs help taking actions that lead to value. | Pay first; earn the stated cash or credit through results, actions, or both. These are different choices. |
| Giveaway | A relevant prize can attract the right buyers. | Pick a real winner. The follow-on offer must be useful. Chance-based promotion rules need checking. |
| Decoy | A basic option helps show the worth of hands-on help. | Keep both basic and premium real. Do not hide the entry offer. |
| Buy X, get Y | A useful larger purchase can raise early cash. | A new label is not always a real price cut. Count every item and its delivery cost. |
| Pay less now or more later | The buyer weighs a lower price against a later charge with a guarantee. | State both prices, terms, due dates, return duties and which guarantee belongs to each. |

Read the chapter you need, not all five: pages30–36,37–44,45–50,51–57,58–62. Pages63–65 show a review ask after giving value. They do not give a sixth detailed price plan.

Use real prices and useful bonuses. Do not inflate a value just to make a discount look large. Prepaid cash must fund the whole service term. (Pages39–41,55–56,130.)

## Offer the Next Thing

- **Classic:** Solve the next real need when it appears. More, better or a useful extra can fit. An optional item is not a need just because a script says it is. (Pages67–75.)
- **Menu:** Remove poor-fit options. Explain what fits and how to use it. Offer real choices and make agreed payment easy. Each tool is optional; not every sale needs all four. (Pages76–84.)
- **Anchor:** Start with a premium you want to sell and can deliver. If its extra features do not matter, show the lower-price fit. If the buyer wants premium, let them choose it; no gasp is required. (Pages85–89.)
- **Rollover:** Apply some prior spend as credit toward a useful next purchase. Credit is not a cash refund or payment of the old provider's debt. Show the new amount due and retain any refund already owed. (Pages90–95.)

A gift-card referral offer may have recipient limits and a minimum purchase. Say so. Do not call unused balances free profit while redemption duties remain. (Image on page94.)

## Respond to a No

Find what the buyer rejects. A payment problem and a value problem call for different changes. Offer another fit if welcome; stop when asked.

- **Payment plan:** Same product, a different payment schedule. Seek more collected sales, not just more signatures. Layaway delivers after full payment; credit delivers before it. Check third-party fees and terms. (Pages101–107.)
- **Trial with conditions:** Start free if the buyer takes agreed actions. The book's trial with penalty differs from paying first and earning money back. Use check-ins and make-up chances to help people get value. The goal is not to earn small penalty fees. (Pages108–115.)
- **Feature downsell:** Lower price by changing quantity, support, access, speed or other optional scope. Learn which features buyers value. A smaller useful plan may also keep a current customer. (Pages116–123.)

Some source scripts take a card before explaining fees or use pressure to gain agreement. That is in the source. It does not make hidden terms sound advice. Explain fees, repeat charges and how to cancel before the buyer agrees. Holding a card is not consent. Removing an extra guarantee does not remove legal rights or basic safety. These are advice boundaries, not invented quotes from Alex.

The book permits planned price tests. Its warning against discounting concerns panic cuts for the same offer in the moment. Do not turn that warning into a ban on testing prices. (Page99.)

## Earn Repeat Sales

Repeat payments need repeat value. A one-time workshop can have a payment plan with an end. That is not an endless membership. (Pages124–125.)

Choose a useful bonus or a clear discount plan. Or waive a fair setup fee for a stated term. Bonuses can reuse work you already do. Use honest prices. A fee waived for a completed term must go away once that term is met. Make it clear how to cancel. An exit interview can help you learn and fix problems; it is not a trap. (Pages127–147.)

Show exactly what the buyer buys. Twelve paid months plus three free is fifteen months. Nine paid plus three free is twelve. A discount spread across twelve bills is a third structure. Monthly and every four weeks are different prices. State renewal terms before agreement. (Pages135–139,154.)

## Check the Cash and the Math

Use a common period and a common group of leads. Count paid buyers, cash collected, delivery costs, refunds, collection losses and work still owed. Compare the old and new path. A lower share of full-pay buyers does not mean lower full-pay conversion from appointments. (Pages102,105.)

Several book examples need care:

- Page68's image gives $2.90 profit from a $0.25 base: 11.6 times. Its prose says $3, which is 12 times. Keep that conflict visible.
- Page74's second price ladder differs between image and prose. Use the relative-gap idea; do not invent a single exact recipe.
- Page124's $40,000 total includes the first $2,000. Do not add it twice. It assumes all forty buyers stay and pay for twenty months.
- Page132's take-rate chart has an unclear price basis. The $399 standalone divided by $199 membership is about 2, not 1.33. A parenthetical monthly figure uses an unexplained period. Do not invent the missing period or promise a take rate.
- Page133's one-in-eight prepay example raises first cash by 50%. Future service costs still matter; it does not prove 50% more profit.
- Page139's four-week example raises receipts about 8.33% over fifty-two weeks. Its roughly 41% profit lift assumes a 20% starting margin and unchanged costs. More fees do not always flow straight to profit.

Use source images for visual lessons, not just text or a verified hash. Image-only material includes pages17,22,42,74,83,94,129,150 and153. Missing indexed text is not missing meaning; inspect the image when needed.

Historical growth, take rates and cancellation rates are examples or author observations. Test the fit; do not turn them into forecasts. Keep exact amounts, units, people and conditions when making the words simpler.

## Secret Sticker Society Case

The [July 10, 2026 Scale or Fail episode](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j2TZMFkj71Q) applies offer, cash and ad ideas to a working digital membership. This is a case map, not a new universal rule or proof of the promised $3m business. Read the relevant raw spans with the scoped video reader. Caption versions differ; originals remain intact.

### Keep the offer order

First, improve the annual offer. Add a useful physical sticker kit so buyers have what they need to use the teaching. The discussion moves from a possible $99 kit to a $149 standalone option included with annual. Its assumed $10 goods plus $10 shipping cost needs checking. Keep the standalone price real and budget for shipping and the year's service. An event-only bonus needs real dates and terms. ([15:35–17:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j2TZMFkj71Q&t=935s), [22:13–22:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j2TZMFkj71Q&t=1333s).)

Pitch annual during the launch. After the cart closes, offer monthly without those bonuses to people who did not buy. “Annual only” is not a permanent ban on monthly. A predicted 50% annual share is not a result. ([17:15–18:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j2TZMFkj71Q&t=1035s).)

Then test the trial. The workshop can give people a useful start inside the $27/month membership. Trial entry is not paid retention: some people leave. Disclose the trial length, charge date, recurring price and how to cancel before they agree. These consent rules are advice boundaries, not a claim that the source gives a full checkout specification. ([18:22–19:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j2TZMFkj71Q&t=1102s).)

The board labels the annual offer test A and trial test B. A $1 trial is an early idea, not the final test design. The closing change holds the upfront price at $10 on both sides, with a trial included in one. That keeps the ads and much of the path comparable. The buyer's recurring duties still differ. ([21:29–22:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j2TZMFkj71Q&t=1289s), [26:01–26:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j2TZMFkj71Q&t=1561s).)

### Keep each number with the right buyers

The worked example uses 100 **ticket buyers**, not 100 clicks. ([19:30–21:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j2TZMFkj71Q&t=1170s).)

Compare both paths over the same group and period. Keep cash separate from lifetime gross profit. Count lost front-end sales, upsells, kit and service costs, refunds, other acquisition costs and when money arrives. A sum that mixes front-end revenue with lifetime gross profit is not a clean profit comparison. The suggested 50–60% gain is not a measured lift. Nor does 100% trial enrollment mean everyone will stay and pay.

The annual price is **$270**, not the newest caption's $217: the older caption and targeted audio check agree. Later $300 examples do not prove a new annual price. A separate “500-ish profit” line lacks a clear basis and must not become $500k annual profit. The source slide shows $800k revenue and $277k profit for the last twelve months. The 5% churn remark gives no period; do not derive lifetime value from it.

### Connect use, proof and ads

Help a new member make a sticker in the first seven days. A secret sticker set rewards that useful action. With permission, their work can supply honest ad material; this is not paying for positive public reviews. Reuse useful live Q&A clips and vary genuine past ad winners. Twenty winners in four settings means eighty versions, not eighty new ideas or guaranteed winners. Add pain-led hooks for buyers who do not yet know the offer; keep useful offer-led ads too. ([9:52–13:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j2TZMFkj71Q&t=592s).)

Delivery could take more buyers in this owner's account. That supports this case's offer-and-traffic focus; another firm may need to fix delivery first. The proposed ad-spend ramp overlaps across weeks and days, so do not add its percentages or copy its $100-per-buyer ceiling as a universal budget.

The annual-share improvement was reported after an earlier call, shown as three months before this session. It does not prove the new kit or trial worked. The closing update reports promising early ad work but gives no final sales, profit or trial results. The next-owner teaser is a different business. ([29:12–29:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j2TZMFkj71Q&t=1752s).)

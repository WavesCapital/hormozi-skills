## Learn Who Buys And Gets Value

When customers exist, learn who spends, stays and is good to serve. Ask why they bought, what they wanted and which useful content or proof helped. Find a few traits that matter. Use them to improve the message, channels and sales path. Do not copy another firm's personal traits or ask for sensitive details with no useful purpose.

The top20% is a way to focus the search, not a rule to fire the other80%. Check service cost, results and cash as well as spending. A bigger customer may get more value from the same work, but size alone does not prove fit or an affordable price. These cost and fit checks qualify the source's selection method.

If there are no customers, start with a field you know and a small group you can help. Serve, learn and revisit the choice when evidence exists. Do not demand a survey of customers who do not exist. A starting group is a hypothesis, not a proven best segment.

## Choose The First Useful Offer

Separate the result from the promotion that gets people to try it. Free or discounted help can build skill, trust and proof. Premium can fit buyers who value the result and a sales path that shows it. Identify who pays and for what; some businesses earn from a partner rather than charge the person they help.

Compare complete paths: buyer, offer, price, message and sales work. A premium offer with weak value does not become a fair test just because its price is higher. Include delivery work and the time until money arrives. Free work is not free to the business.

If even free gets no response, check whether people want it, believe it, and actually see it. Give a true reason for a generous offer. Use real details from buyers' lives; do not invent a vivid pain point.

Discount a useful, understood first step when that fits. Repeated discounts on the main service may teach buyers to wait. Give value before the next sale; a stored card makes payment easier but does not grant permission to charge it. Use qualified staff for suitable first-step work so expert time goes where it helps, without handing them work they cannot perform.

Source: bonus chapters,3–12 and17–24. Page17's image says to compare whole strategies. The draft's discount sizes, local-gym tests and reported lead-cost gains are examples or preferences, not required prices or promised results.

## Use Filters To Improve The Whole Result

Count useful leads, buyers and money left after the work—not just the qualified percentage. The bonus example spends$1,000 for500leads with250qualified, versus200leads with160qualified. That is$4 versus$6.25 of ad spend per qualified lead. It favors the first path on that limited measure. Handling cost, buyer value, closing, delivery and available time can change the decision.

The bonus chapters offer five ways to filter: qualifications, requested information, added steps, required content and ad length. Each can lose good buyers too. A long required video may fit some large audiences; the source says low volume can make it unjustified. Use a step because it helps the buyer or solves a real fit or capacity problem, not because more steps sound better.

The short Offers chapter found that many of its best customers had seen at least two long pieces of content. That is not proof that every buyer must watch two videos. Give relevant, genuinely useful help and check whether it improves the whole path. Do not remove a useful qualification just to raise lead count either.

Source: bonus chapters,14–17 and20–22; short Offers chapter,5–7. Buying-path correlations and the repeated gym tests are not independent causal proof.

## Keep Examples And Choices In Context

Repeat sales may improve by making the product better OR by finding people it already fits. The short chapter focuses on the second choice, without ruling out the first. Its enterprise-customer example does not settle another firm's service cost, payment delays or needs. (Short chapter, page7 image.)

Do not copy the short chapter's multipliers as forecasts. If20% of a fixed customer count brings80% of revenue, giving all customers that group's average implies4times revenue, not5. Page4's move from5% to7% conversion is a40% relative lift, but its monthly examples do not match its claimed annual fees. Preserve those source errors; recalculate on the actual period when the example matters.

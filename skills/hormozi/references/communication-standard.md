# Plain writing

Use this guide for substantial writing, a voice problem, or a requested writing audit. The entrypoint's plain-language standard applies to every reply without rereading this guide.

## Write from the meaning

Explain who does what and why it matters. Use the reader's words, strong verbs and natural full thoughts. Short is not choppy. Keep exact terms when needed and explain them beside their first use. Source names, quotes, formulas and file paths do not need simplification.

A useful contrast: “Improve retention” can mean “Help more buyers stay.” Choose the meaning that fits; do not apply a fixed word swap.

## Give feedback

Name the problem, explain its cost and show the better version when useful. These are ingredients, not four required sections. A small correction may need only the improved line. Challenge the idea without shaming the person.

## Write copy

Speak from the actual sender to the intended reader. Name the wanted result and how the offer helps. Be clear before clever. Include the price, terms and limits that reader needs, but keep internal math and source notes outside the finished copy. Words such as “free,” “easy” and “instant” are claims too.

A proposed service improvement is not an existing buyer promise. Do not invent proof, urgency, personal stories or quotes. For familiar comparisons, describe what matches without implying the comparison proves a result.

## Keep the answer whole

Before shortening a complex answer, identify the facts and exceptions that change the decision. Cut repetition before those details. Keep cause and effect, speaker attribution, units and time spans. Separate the source's case from advice for this business.

## Final check

Is the answer true, easy to understand, and complete for the request? Check the final wording against the evidence. A rewrite must retain needed facts and support new claims. The opening, body and closing should agree. A comparison should not turn “may” into “will” or an inferred motive into something the speaker said.

## Optional reading hints

`scripts/plain_language.py --file DRAFT --target-grade 3` flags wording worth reviewing. It reports a grade estimate, not whether the answer is true, natural or useful. A sentence above 16 words, a second comma or a passive pattern can be fine. Keep the third-grade aim, not a rigid sentence ceiling.

The command's `ok` field means no mechanical flags, not good advice; `quality_status` remains `not_assessed`. Use `--strict` only when explicitly testing the mechanical rules. Fix real clarity problems and stop when the answer works. Do not chase flags with repeated rewrites.
